Estimating and checking answers

An estimate is a quick answer that is close enough to show whether an exact answer is sensible. Estimating helps you spot mistakes, such as a missing digit or a decimal point in the wrong place. You do not need a calculator to do it.

Step 1: round to easy numbers

Round each number to one non-zero digit, or to the nearest ten or hundred, so the sum or product is easy to work out in your head.

Worked examples

Addition

Estimate 398 + 207. Round to 400 + 200 = 600. The exact answer is 605. The estimate is close, so 605 is sensible.

Multiplication

Estimate 19 × 21. Round both to 20: 20 × 20 = 400. The exact answer is 399. Rounding up one number and down the other can give a very close estimate.

Estimate 4.8 × 3.1. Round to 5 × 3 = 15. The exact answer is 14.88, so the estimate is close.

Division

Estimate 612 ÷ 19. Round to 600 ÷ 20 = 30. Check by long division or by multiplying: 19 × 32 = 608, and 608 + 4 = 612, so the answer is 32 remainder 4. The estimate of 30 is close to 32.

Step 2: check the answer

An estimate tells you whether an exact answer is in the right region. Then use one or more of these checks:

  • Size. Is the answer bigger or smaller than the estimate by a reasonable amount? For 37 × 12, the estimate is 40 × 10 = 400. The answer 344 is too small, and 37 × 12 = 444 is close to 400.
  • Last digit. The last digit of a product depends only on the last digits of the numbers. For 47 × 8, the last digit of 7 × 8 = 56 is 6, and 376 ends in 6, so the answer could be right. This check does not prove the answer is right, only that it could be.
  • Reverse the operation. If you divided, multiply back. If you subtracted, add back. For 612 ÷ 19 = 32 remainder 4, check 19 × 32 + 4 = 612.
  • Sign. Adding a positive number makes a total bigger. Multiplying two negatives gives a positive. Check that the sign of the answer matches what you expect.
Estimates are only a check. If the exact answer is far from the estimate, look for a mistake in the working, such as a carry that was missed or a decimal point in the wrong place.

Common mistakes

  • Rounding one number up and another number up too, which can make the estimate much bigger than it should be. Round the numbers in a way that keeps the estimate close.
  • Trusting a check that only tests one thing, such as the last digit. Use two checks when the answer matters.
  • Forgetting the order of operations. Work out multiplication and division before addition and subtraction, as in order of operations.

Check yourself

  1. Estimate 49 + 31 by rounding to the nearest ten. What do you get?
  2. Estimate 83 × 9 by rounding 83 to 80. Is the exact answer near 720?
  3. Is 25 × 16 equal to 460? Check by estimating and by working it out.

Answers: 80 (the exact answer is 80); yes, the exact answer is 747, which is close to 720; no, 25 × 16 = 400, because 25 × 4 = 100 and 100 × 4 = 400.
