Prime numbers

A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and itself. Numbers that are not prime, and are greater than 1, are called composite. For example, 6 is composite because 2 × 3 = 6.

Primes up to 30

The primes up to 30 are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23 and 29. That is ten primes. The number 1 is not prime, because it has only one divisor. The number 2 is the only even prime, since every other even number is divisible by 2.

Testing a number

To check whether a number n is prime, you only need to try dividing it by the primes up to the square root of n. If none of them divides n exactly, n is prime.

Take 91. Its square root is about 9.5, so try 2, 3, 5 and 7. Dividing 91 by 7 gives exactly 13 (7 × 13 = 91), so 91 is composite.

Take 97. Its square root is about 9.8, so try 2, 3, 5 and 7. None divides 97 exactly, so 97 is prime.

Finding primes with a sieve

The sieve method is an old way to list primes. Write down the numbers from 2 to 30. Keep 2, and cross out every later multiple of 2. Move to the next number left uncrossed, which is 3, and cross out its later multiples. Repeat with 5, then 7. The numbers that remain are the primes. Crossing out is the same as saying "this has a smaller divisor".

There are endlessly many primes

The list never ends. A short argument from ancient mathematics shows this: if you multiply a finite list of primes and add 1, the result has a prime factor that was not on the list. So no finite list can contain every prime.

Quick check

Which of 51 and 53 is prime? (Answer: 53. The number 51 is 3 × 17. The number 53 has a square root of about 7.3, and none of 2, 3, 5 or 7 divides it.)
