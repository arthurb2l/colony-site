Counting in octal

Octal is base 8. It uses eight digits, 0 to 7. Each place is worth eight times the place to its right: 1, 8, 64, 512, and so on.

How to read one

Take 17 in octal. The right place is worth 1 and the left place is worth 8. So 17 octal = 1 × 8 + 7 × 1 = 15 in our everyday counting.

Counting in octal

Count from 0 to 7, the same as usual. Then the count runs out of digits, so it carries into the next place: after 7 comes 10, which means one eight and no ones.

Octal and binary

Each octal digit matches exactly three binary digits, because 8 is 2 × 2 × 2. To convert, split the binary number into groups of three from the right, then read each group as one octal digit. For 101110: the groups are 101 and 110, which are 5 and 6. So 101110 in binary is 56 in octal. Check: 56 octal = 5 × 8 + 6 = 46, and 101110 in binary = 32 + 8 + 4 + 2 = 46.

Octal is a short way to write binary, like hexadecimal but with groups of three bits. For the base 2 rules, see the binary page.

Where octal shows up: Unix permissions

On Unix-style systems, a file's permissions are often written as three octal digits, such as 755 or 644. Each digit covers three permissions, each with a value:

PermissionValue
read (r)4
write (w)2
execute (x)1

A digit is the sum of the permissions it grants. So 7 = 4 + 2 + 1 means read, write and execute. 6 = 4 + 2 means read and write. 5 = 4 + 1 means read and execute. 4 means read only.

The three digits are for the owner, the group and everyone else. So 755 means the owner has rwx, the group has r-x, and everyone else has r-x. In letters: rwxr-xr-x. The number 644 means rw-r--r--.
