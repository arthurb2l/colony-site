Reading bar charts and tables

A chart shows numbers as pictures. A table shows the same numbers as text. Both can mislead if you do not check what they show. This page explains the parts of a bar chart, how to read a value from it, and how to compare bars fairly. All the numbers here are made up for practice.

The parts of a bar chart

  • Title says what the chart is about.
  • Labels name each bar, such as a day or a shop.
  • Scale is the set of marks along one edge, such as 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10. It tells you how much each bar length means.
  • Bars are drawn from the zero line up to (or across to) the value they show.

Each bar length is a value. Here the scale is 20 pixels for each apple, so the Monday bar is 6 × 20 = 120 pixels long. To read a value from a chart with no numbers on the bars, find where the bar ends on the scale. If the bar ends halfway between the 4 and the 6 marks, the value is 5.

Check the scale first

Before you compare bars, look at where the scale starts and how it goes up. Most bar charts start at zero, and the bars grow from zero. This keeps the lengths honest.

Now take two shops that sold 48 and 42 phones in a month. Here are two versions of the same bars.

Version 1: the scale starts at 0 and goes to 50. The bar for 42 is a little shorter than the bar for 48. The difference is 48 − 42 = 6, which is 6 ÷ 48 = 12.5% of the larger number.

Version 2: the scale starts at 40 and goes to 50. Now the 48 bar is 8 units tall above the line, and the 42 bar is only 2 units tall. The 48 bar looks four times as tall as the 42 bar. The real difference did not change, but the picture now makes it look much larger.

When a bar chart does not start at zero, the length of the bars stops matching the values. Read the numbers on the scale, not only the lengths.

Comparing bars

To compare two bars, find both values and then work out the difference or the ratio. Use the apple chart above.

  • Tuesday had 9 apples and Monday had 6. The difference is 9 − 6 = 3 more apples on Tuesday.
  • Tuesday had 9 apples and Friday had 3. Tuesday was 9 ÷ 3 = 3 times Friday.
  • Wednesday had 4 apples. That is 4 ÷ 8 = one half of Thursday, which had 8.

A bar that is twice as long as another shows twice the value only when the bars start from zero. Check the scale first.

Parts of a whole

Sometimes you want to know what share one bar is of the total. Add all the values first. For the five days: 6 + 9 + 4 + 8 + 3 = 30 apples in total.

Tuesday's share is 9 out of 30, which is 9 ÷ 30 = 0.3, or 30%. See fractions and percent for how these forms match.

Typical values

The mean of the five days is 30 ÷ 5 = 6 apples. The median is the middle value when the days are sorted: 3, 4, 6, 8, 9. The middle one is 6. A bar chart lets you see the mean and the median at a glance, because they are the bars that sit near the middle.

Tables and charts

A table gives the exact numbers. A chart gives the shape: which days are high, which are low, and how big the gaps look. The same apple numbers in a table:

Use a table when you need exact values. Use a chart when you want to see the pattern quickly. If a chart has no numbers, look for a table next to it or a note that gives the values.

Quick check

  1. On the apple chart, how many apples were sold on Thursday? (Answer: 8)
  2. What is the total for the five days? (Answer: 30)
  3. What share of the total was sold on Friday? (Answer: 3 out of 30, which is 10%)
  4. A bar chart's scale starts at 40 instead of 0. Do the bar lengths still match the values? (Answer: no. Only the differences shown on the scale are fair to compare.)
