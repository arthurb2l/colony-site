Bit shifts explained

A shift moves every bit of a binary number a fixed number of places left or right. The empty places are filled with 0 (or, for right shifts of negative numbers in some systems, with copies of the sign bit; this page only uses positive numbers). Shifts pair naturally with the rules on the bitwise page.

The two directions

  • Shift left moves each bit one place toward the left and adds a 0 on the right. Each shift left doubles the number.
  • Shift right moves each bit one place toward the right and drops the last bit on the right. Each shift right halves the number and throws away any remainder.

Why the 1 in 1101 disappears

Shifting 1101 right by one place moves the bits to give 110, and the final 1 falls off the end. Since 13 is 6 times 2 plus 1, dropping that 1 leaves 6. That is why a right shift gives the whole-number part of a division by 2: 13 divided by 2 is 6 with a remainder of 1.

A pattern to check

For a left shift by n places, the result is the original number times 2 to the power n. Check it on the table: 5 times 2 is 10, and 5 times 4 is 20. For a right shift by n places, the result is the original number divided by 2 to the power n, with the remainder dropped. 13 divided by 4 is 3 with a remainder, which matches the 11 found above.

Practice

  1. Shift 111 (7) left by 1. What is the result in binary and in decimal?
  2. Shift 10100 (20) right by 2. What is the result in binary and in decimal?
  3. Shift 1011 (11) left by 2. What is the result in binary and in decimal?

Answers

  1. 1110, which is 14. The shift adds a 0 on the right, and 7 times 2 is 14.
  2. 101, which is 5. Dropping the last two bits from 10100 leaves 101.
  3. 101100, which is 44. Adding two 0s to the right of 1011 gives 101100, and 11 times 4 is 44.
